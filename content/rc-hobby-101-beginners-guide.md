If you're an adult getting into RC for the first time, the amount of conflicting advice online is genuinely worse than most hobbies. This page is the one we'd want a friend to send us before spending any money.

## Cars, planes, or boats -- how to actually choose

**Pick RC cars if:** you want the lowest learning curve, you want to be driving (not slowly learning to fly) within your first session, and you have some open ground nearby -- a driveway, a park, a gravel lot.

**Pick RC boats if:** you have regular access to a lake or large pond, you want something you can be reasonably competent at from day one, and you like the idea of a hobby that doesn't involve crashing into things as a normal part of learning.

**Pick RC planes if:** you're specifically drawn to flying (not just "RC vehicles" generally), you're willing to accept a real learning curve with real crash risk in the first few sessions, and ideally you have access to an actual flying field or local club.

Most people who get seriously into the RC hobby long-term end up owning more than one category eventually. But your first purchase should match the thing you're actually excited about, not the thing that's theoretically "easiest" -- excitement is what gets you through the inevitable first-month frustration of learning a new mechanical skill.

## Hobby-grade vs. toy-grade, in plain terms

This is the single most important concept for a beginner to understand, across all three categories.

**Toy-grade** vehicles are sealed, non-serviceable units. Cheap, fine for a genuinely casual "will my kid like this" test, but when something breaks -- and with any RC vehicle, something eventually breaks -- you throw the whole thing away.

**Hobby-grade** vehicles are built from replaceable, often upgradeable parts. More expensive upfront, but a $300 hobby-grade RC car with $150 of crash damage over a year is a $150 repair. A $300 toy-grade car with the same damage is a $300 trip to the trash can and back to the store.

For an adult with any actual intention of sticking with the hobby, hobby-grade is worth the extra money essentially every time.

## What it actually costs to get started

Sticker prices are usually just the beginning. Budget in the following, regardless of which category you choose:

- **The vehicle itself** (RTR/ready-to-run is the right starting point for beginners in all three categories -- don't start with a kit you build yourself unless the build itself is the appeal).
- **A second battery.** Nearly every RTR vehicle ships with one battery and enough runtime for 10-20 minutes before you're waiting around to charge again. A second battery roughly doubles usable time per outing.
- **A better charger**, eventually. Bundled chargers are usually slow and basic. Not a day-one purchase, but budget for it once you're hooked.
- **Spare parts specific to your model** -- a few of the parts most likely to break (an A-arm, a propeller, a hull pin) bought in advance so a bad crash doesn't end your session for the day.

## The single biggest mistake beginners make

Buying based on top speed or size alone, without checking parts availability first. Search "[the exact model you're considering] replacement parts" before buying, not after. If you can't easily find a real parts ecosystem, you're looking at a toy that will become disposable the first time something breaks -- no matter how "hobby-grade" the marketing sounds.
