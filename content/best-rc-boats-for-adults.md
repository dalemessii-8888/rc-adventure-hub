RC boats get less attention than RC cars and planes, but they're arguably the lowest-hassle way into the hobby -- no crash-prone flight envelope to learn, no gravel to tear up suspension parts, just point it at open water and go. The main things that separate a good adult RC boat from a frustrating one are hull self-righting, brushless power, and knowing what water you're actually going to run it in before you buy.

## What actually matters

**Self-righting hulls.** At speed, RC boats flip. It's not a matter of if, it's when. A self-righting hull can flip itself back over (usually by briefly reversing the motor) without you needing to wade in or paddle a kayak out to flip it by hand. For anything you plan to run fast, this single feature is worth prioritizing over almost anything else on a spec sheet.

**Brushless vs. brushed.** Same logic as RC cars -- brushless costs more up front but is faster, more efficient, and needs less maintenance. Given that boats are often run on lakes (i.e., not a five-minute walk from home if something goes wrong), the reliability of brushless is worth the premium for anything beyond a first "is this fun" purchase.

**Match the boat to your water.** A boat built for pond/lake use will get overwhelmed in any real chop or current. If you're planning to run on a larger lake or anything with boat wake, size up.

**Range and recovery.** Figure out your recovery plan before you need it -- a boat that loses power in the middle of a lake with no wind to blow it back to shore is a real and common way to lose a boat. Some hobbyists keep a cheap secondary "rescue boat" or a long length of paracord and a throwable weight on hand for exactly this scenario.

## Before you take any RC boat to open water

Check your local regulations -- some lakes, reservoirs, and parks restrict or ban RC boats, particularly gas/nitro models, and some require registration for anything above a certain speed or size. It's a five-minute search that avoids a genuinely annoying conversation with a park ranger.
